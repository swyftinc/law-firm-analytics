# Reading list

None of the ideas in these skills are ours. They come from people who spent careers teaching how to look at numbers fairly and show them clearly. To go deeper than a skill can take you, start here, and if you only read three, read the first three.

Titles and authors are listed as recommended reading. No author or publisher has endorsed or sponsored this repo. Each entry says what the skills took from the book, in our words. `references/foundations.md` inside each skill maps every rule to its source, with links.

## Start here

**Storytelling with Data**, Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic (Wiley, 2015). A good on-ramp for anyone who presents numbers to people who aren't analysts. Start with the question and the one thing you want the reader to take away, remove clutter, and use color to point at what matters. This is where the "one accent color" rule and the briefing's single big idea come from. Her later books, *Let's Practice!* (2019), *Storytelling with You* (2022) and *Storytelling with Data: Before & After* (2025, with Mike Cisneros and Alex Velez), add exercises and redesigns. The before and after picture in our README follows the makeover format of the last one.

**The Visual Display of Quantitative Information**, Edward Tufte (Graphics Press, 1983, second edition 2001). A foundation text on graphical integrity. Show the data, cut ink that carries none, and keep a chart's visual effect in proportion to the effect in the data. Small multiples, the same small chart repeated for comparison, come from here, and our zero-baseline rule applies his test that a chart's visual effect should match the effect in the data. *Beautiful Evidence* (2006) gives us sparklines.

**Naked Statistics**, Charles Wheelan (W. W. Norton, 2013). Statistics explained without the dread. It is why the skills lead with medians for skewed money and time, and why a small sample is called too thin.

## Reasoning about numbers

**The Signal and the Noise**, Nate Silver (Penguin Press, 2012). Telling real change from noise, and weighing new evidence against what you already believed. The "normal range" label and the briefing's habit of stating causes as hypotheses come from this way of thinking.

**Data Science for Business**, Foster Provost and Tom Fawcett (O'Reilly, 2013). Its idea of "data-analytic thinking" is framing a business problem as a question data can answer. Each item in the Monday briefing ends in something specific to look at.

**Everybody Lies**, Seth Stephens-Davidowitz (Dey Street Books, 2017). What people say can differ from what they do. It is why a lead source built only from an intake question gets less trust than a tracked source.

**Weapons of Math Destruction**, Cathy O'Neil (Crown, 2016). How opaque, large-scale scoring harms people. It is why every number here traces back to its inputs, why there is no invented "health score," and why a number about one attorney is framed as a question to ask and never as a verdict.

## Designing charts and dashboards

**The Functional Art**, Alberto Cairo (New Riders, 2012), and **The Truthful Art** (New Riders, 2016). In the first, function constrains form, and decoration mustn't cover the information. The five qualities of a good one, truthful, functional, beautiful, insightful and enlightening, are the standard behind the five questions in `chart-doctrine.md`.

**The Big Book of Dashboards**, Steve Wexler, Jeffrey Shaffer and Andy Cotgreave (Wiley, 2017). Dashboards built for real scenarios. The Monday briefing is built for one scenario, the Monday meeting. Its chapters on pies and donuts and on red and green are where to read more on those two topics. Our own rules, few slices and an arrow and a word beside every status color, are built on the same worries.

## Running a law firm by the numbers

**Law Firm KPIs**, Mori Kabiri (2025). A handbook of more than 80 metrics for pricing, productivity and profitability, with alternative fee arrangements. We drew on public sources for the standard definitions of utilization, realization, collection and lockup, and did not copy the book's list or arrangement. Kabiri's *Legal Operations KPIs* (2023, second edition 2026) is the in-house counterpart.

**The Power of Legal Project Management**, Susan Raridon Lambreth and David A. Rueff Jr. (American Bar Association, first edition 2014, second edition 2021 or 2022, depending on the source). Treating a matter as something you scope, budget, run and review. It is the root of the flat fee metrics that compare hours with the fee.

**Running Legal Like a Business**, Connie Brenton and Susan Raridon Lambreth (Practising Law Institute, 2021). Written for corporate legal departments, with a focus on reporting on legal activity and choosing the right metrics for day-to-day decisions. We borrow the stance, not the metrics.

**Data-Driven Law**, edited by Edward J. Walters (Auerbach Publications, 2019). Firms do better when they collect and standardize matter data over the whole life of a matter. It is why each metric here is defined once, with its inputs stated.
